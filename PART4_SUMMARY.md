# Part 4: Where the Over-Claiming Concentrates


## The finding

Companies tend to over-claim more on the values their **industry expects** them to hold. But that's not really the driver. The sharper pattern is this: companies **back up** the expected values that are **costly or regulated**, and **over-claim** the expected values that are **soft and image-driven**. An energy company genuinely engages with the environment in its filings, because climate is material, regulated, and litigated for it. But it leans on softer themes like heritage and innovation that nothing forces it to substantiate. So the real distinction isn't "expected vs not." It's **cheap-and-unenforced vs costly-and-enforced.**

## Process

We started with a natural hypothesis: the values an industry is *expected* to profess are the cheapest to fake, because social pressure makes you say them while nothing makes you back them up. Using the Part 3 index, we split each company's over-claiming into the values its industry expects (the ones most of its peers profess) versus the rest.

**The hypothesis held: four of five industries over-claim more on their expected values** (technology and healthcare most strongly; energy and consumer next; financial firms were the lone exception, actually over-claiming *less* on their expected value). About two-thirds of companies followed the pattern. So "industry-expected" is part of the story.

## The issue

The energy industry was supposed to be the main issue. Surely environment, the value energy is most expected to profess, would be its biggest over-claim. Instead it sat **near the bottom (8th of 11 values)**. Looking at why was the whole insight: energy companies' filings *actually discuss the environment a lot*, because for an oil company climate is a real, regulated, litigated risk they're compelled to address. The values they over-claim instead are the soft, unfalsifiable ones, **heritage, innovation, human progress**, that a filing has no reason to detail.

When we tested that across all five industries, it held cleanly: the expected values companies over-claim most are the soft image ones; the expected values they back up are the concrete, filing-heavy ones (operations, workforce, customers). **Energy wasn't special. It was just the first place we noticed a rule that holds everywhere.** The actionable version: be skeptical of a company's loudest claims on *soft* values (legacy, innovation, "improving lives"); give more weight to its claims on *costly, regulated* values, where the filing has to put up or shut up.

## The research-integrity note 

Our stand-in for "costly/regulated" is *how much the filing discusses a value*, and our scoring method mechanically gives a smaller over-claim score to anything the filing discusses a lot. So "soft values are over-claimed more" and "rarely-filed values score higher by construction" are, in this analysis, **almost the same thing** (they move together very tightly). We genuinely believe the two readings coincide: a filing detailing a value at length really is what "backing it up" looks like. But we can only honestly say we've **described** the pattern, not **proven** the mechanism behind it. There's also a second unresolved confounder: a value being "expected" and being "over-claimed" might both just track what's broadly fashionable, rather than industry conformity specifically. Both caveats are why this is framed as exploratory.
